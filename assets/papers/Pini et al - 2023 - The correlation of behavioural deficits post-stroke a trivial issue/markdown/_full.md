We read with great interest the paper by Sperber et al . 1 about the effects of lesion anatomy on the description of post-stroke behavioural deficits. The authors:

'investigated the dimensionality of artificial simulated neuropsychological profiles that exclusively contained independent fundamental cognitive functions  without  any  underlying  low-dimensional  cognitive  architecture. Still, the anatomy of stroke lesions alone was sufficient to create a dependence between variables that allowed a low-dimensional description of the data with principal component analysis.' (from their abstract).

Accordingly, they claimed that the correlation of behavioural deficits  post-stroke,  found  in  several  previous  studies  including ours, 2,3 is trivially explained by the overlap of stroke lesions and by their volume.

However, the fact that a model can generate a low-dimensional result that does not reflect the underlying cognitive architecture is of no value if the model is based on very unrealistic assumptions and is contradicted by established results in the stroke literature. Although the authors suggest that even unrealistic models can be informative, our view is that their model is simply too far removed from  any  plausible,  empirically  supported  realization  of  brainbehaviour relationships to be useful.

Two general assumptions allowed the authors to compute the dimensionality  of  post-stroke  behavioural  deficits  strictly  from grey matter (GM) damage providing the basis for their claim that the low dimensionality of post-stroke deficits does not reflect an underlying cognitive architecture.

- (i) Each GM region mediates an independent cognitive function, i.e. there is a 1:1 relationship between GM regions and cognitive functions. If two regions are damaged by a lesion two independent functions are impaired. This  very  strong  assumption  allowed  the  authors  to  ignore  behaviour completely and focus entirely on lesion damage. However, the authors did not provide any biological justification or supporting empirical evidence and neglected both one area-to-many cognitive processes as well as many areas-to-one cognitive processes, both supported by empirical evidence. 4 Back  in  1990,  Mesulam  wrote  that  'complex  behavior  is mapped at the level of multifocal neural systems rather than specific
- anatomical  sites,  giving  rise  to  brain-behavior  relationships  that  are both localized and distributed'. 5
- (ii) Damage to white matter (WM) does not result in behavioural deficits, which in conjunction with the first assumption, allowed the authors to focus exclusively on GM damage as a proxy for behavioural deficits. Again, no biological justification or supporting evidence was given for this assumption,  which  is  clearly  contradicted  by  empirical  results  showing that damage to WM regions is a good predictor of post-stroke behavioural deficits, including the number of affected functional domains. 2,6 In fact, the lesion frequency map in Fig. 1 from Sperber et al . 1 clearly shows that most lesions damage the subcortical WM and GM, in line with previous studies. 2,3

In addition to the above assumptions, the authors made several algorithmic decisions that were adopted for computational convenience. No justifications were given for these decisions based on the biology of either cognitive deficits or lesion anatomy.

- (iii)  The magnitude of a cognitive deficit was simply assumed to be proportional to the amount of GM damage in the associated region. It appears that this assumption, along with others (e.g. the irrelevance of WM damage) were adopted to facilitate the use of principal component analysis (PCA) on GM damage as a proxy for a PCA on behaviour (we write 'appears' because the specific application of PCA was not well specified in the article or its supplementary material, which was surprising given the prominence  assigned  to  PCA  in  the  authors'  introduction  and  discussion). Although the authors' claim that a PCA of GM damage is a proxy for a PCA of behaviour could at least have been partly examined by comparing their simulated results with the empirical neuropsychological measures, no relevant analyses were reported.

In  addition  to  the  proportional  damage  assumption,  Sperber et al . 1 claimed that correlations between deficits depend critically on lesion volume ('The association problem is related to the size of lesions', page 12), ignoring clear empirical evidence to the contrary. Corbetta et al . 2 measured the influence of lesion volume on the severity of behavioural deficits within and across domains of function, and their correlation. They found that lesion volume predicted only ∼ 1-15% of motor, language and verbal memory scores,

(iv) Several algorithmic decisions changed the structure of the data and may have artificially forced a (relatively) low dimensionality of lesion anatomy. First,  the  authors  flipped  all  lesions  onto  the  same  hemisphere and second, rather than working in a voxel space, the authors embedded their lesions in 94 parcels taken from the BN-246 atlas. The dimensionality  of  stroke  lesion  anatomy  has  been  reported  in  several  studies conducted  in  voxel  space.  Thiebaut  de  Schotten et  al . 7 showed  that 30 components explained 70% of the lesion anatomy in a very large sample of 1300 lesions. Salvalaggio et al . 8 found between n = 15 and n = 58 principal components (PCs) explaining > 95% of the variance in groups of  stroke  lesions  varying  from n = 31  to n = 116.  In  contrast,  Sperber et al . 1 computed the overlap between lesions in voxel space and 94 parcels from the BN-246 atlas. The authors also selected for their analysis only regions with at least 20% overlap across lesions and ignored small lesions as 'clinically irrelevant'. These decisions were not well justified and may have resulted in an underestimate of the spatial variability of stroke  lesions  as  well  as  exaggerating  the  'association  problem'  that was described in the manuscript.

(v) Finally, the authors' conclusions depend on taking at face value the specific numbers yielded by their simulation. Those numbers varied depending on how the simulation was conducted, with a dimensionality ranging from 1

to 4 if six brain regions were selected and from 4 to 8 if 20 regions were selected. The authors did not indicate why only a small number of regions (i.e. 20 or less) were selected for their simulation or provide any justification for their choice, yet apparently, simulations involving a larger number of regions would have yielded larger estimates of dimensionality.

Figure 1 shows the impact of the above decisions on the results. The dataset comes from Bisogno et al . 3 and consists of n = 168 patients and 31 behavioural scores. First, both placing the lesions in a parcellated space and flipping the lesions decrease the dimensionality of the components accounting for lesion location variability.  Interestingly,  unflipped  lesions  in  voxel  space  require  the highest number of components to be explained, and flipped lesions in parcel space the lowest number of components (similar to behaviour). Second, behaviour is summarized by the lowest number of components clearly showing that it cannot be explained by lesion anatomy (Fig. 1).

Far from being trivially explained by GM anatomy or lesion volume, the low dimensionality of behavioural deficits post-stroke remains  a  fascinating  topic  of  investigation.  In  stroke  the  low dimensionality of behaviour is becoming an accepted biomarker for  large  scale  studies  (epidemiology,  genetics,  treatment). 9,10 Moreover,  a  low  dimensionality  of  behavioural  deficits  is  not unique to stroke, but it has also been reported in degenerative conditions that certainly cannot be explained by lesion anatomy. A direct  comparison  between  post-stroke  aphasia  (PSA)  and  primary progressive aphasia (PPA-a form of aphasia due to neurodegenerative mechanisms) reported that cognitive and language impairments could be captured by four main dimensions accounting for ∼ 70% of variance in patient performance. 10 Considering the distinct pathophysiological  mechanisms  that  underlie  PSA  and  PPA,  it seems unlikely that the observed results can be solely attributed to atrophy or lesion anatomy.

In  conclusion,  we  respectfully  submit  that  the  current  work does not provide any basis for concluding that the correlation of post-stroke  behavioural  deficits  is  explained  by  lesion  anatomy alone. An adequate quantitative account of the low dimensionality of post-stroke behavioural variability and correlation will require much more realistic multivariate models that can be justified biologically and are consistent with the stroke literature.
