### Section a. State-of-the-art and objectives

---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Focal brain disorders, including stroke, trauma, and epilepsy, are the main causes of disability and loss of productivity  in  the  world,  and  carry  a cumulative cost in Europe of about 500 billion euro/year. Physicians still largely diagnose and treat these conditions as if they  were  caused  by  local  dysfunction due to the pathological process. However,  there  is  growing  evidence that, in most focal brain disorders, clinical  symptoms  reflect  widespread network-related abnormalities that could be ameliorated by normalizing patterns of activity through 'circuit-based' stimulation.  This  form  of  therapy  is currently  limited  by  numerous  factors: lack of knowledge about the underlying circuitries and their relevance to symptoms; a strategy for mapping circuit  abnormalities  in  single  patients; and, most importantly, a principled understanding  of  where  and  how  to stimulate the brain.

NEMESIS from the Ancient Greek ' Νέμεσις'  is  the  goddess  who  enacts retribution  against  those  who  succumb to hubris. In Greek the word translates in 'give what is due'. NEMESIS aims to 'give to an injured brain what is missing', i.e., restore through stimulation normal activity in dysfunctional brain circuitries. By

### Why is this proposal beyond the edge, and does it represent a breakthrough?

- NEMESIS will provide for the first time a multi-scale, quantitative, and mechanistic understanding of the effects of focal injury (stroke) on the brain. It will show that neurological deficits do not depend on local damage but distributed patterns of abnormal activity across many brain networks.
- NEMESIS  will  employ  computational  modelling  in  combination  with  animal  models  to  test  novel strategies for high precision individualized circuit stimulation to treat neurological deficits, solving the critical problem of where and how to stimulate.
- NEMESIS' circuit stimulation strategy for focal injury can be extended to other focal and possibly nonfocal  pathologies  in  which  brain  activity  is  altered  (e.g.,  degenerative  conditions,  drug  addiction  or psychiatric disorders).
- NEMESIS will synergize neurology, neurophysiology, neurobiology, physics, computational neuroscience, and neuro-technology) to address these fundamental issues in neuroscience and health.

### Why is this proposal high risk and high gain?

- The  proposal  is high  risk because  of  the  strong  hypothesis  that  focal  injuries  induce  structural  and functional  disconnection  of  brain  networks,  and  that  functional  disconnection  relates  to  a  simple neurophysiological  mechanism:  sleep-like  slow  waves  generated  near  the  lesion  that  propagate  and functionally impair normally connected brain regions. Moreover, we assume that this mechanism can be modulated by localized brain stimulation to restore function. Despite the high risk, our results demonstrate that focal lesions produce relatively few (low dimensional) spatial patterns of functional disconnection, therefore amenable to modelling and modulation. Moreover, our novel results since the last submission (2020)  strongly  strengthen  the  main  hypothesis.  However,  if  the  central  hypothesis  were  not  correct, alternative mechanisms of disconnection can be studied and used for circuit-based stimulation.
- The proposal is high gain because  brain  disorders  are  extremely  common,  and  a  general  strategy  for circuit-based treatment has enormous potential for health and society. In Europe 164 million people, 38% of the population, suffer from some form of mental disorder, with an economic impact of 798 billion euros. Neuropsychiatric disorders are now more common than cardiovascular disorders. 25% of brain diseases are  neurological  with  migraine,  stroke,  trauma,  dementia,  and  epilepsy,  the  most  common.  All  these disorders are characterized by abnormal patterns of activity in circuitries that are injured (stroke, trauma), abnormally developed (autism, epilepsy, psychiatric disorders), degenerated (Alzheimer or Parkinson's disease),  or  misfiring  (migraine,  drug  addiction).  Our  strategy  is  flexible  and  can  be  adapted  to  other models of disconnection or circuit alteration.

### Why is this proposal of high impact and synergistic?

- NEMESIS would not have been possible 5 years  ago.  Maurizio  *Corbetta*, Marcello Massimini, Mavi Sanchez-Vives, and Gustavo Deco have developed in the last half decade independent bodies of work that form 'four  pillars' on  which  this  proposal  stands,  and  that,  now  and  in  Europe,  can  be  synergized theoretically and experimentally to change the way we think and treat brain injuries. Furthermore, the four PIs have had previous collaborations/publications in pairs, which facilitates this synergistic project.
- NEMESIS is high impact because it connects different domains of knowledge (focal injury, sleep, brain networks) by providing a mechanistic understanding of focal lesions on whole brain activity.
- NEMESIS provides a new strategy for brain rehabilitation by harnessing different behaviourally relevant brain signals to guide circuit stimulation. This contrasts with current strategies that deal with the brain as a black box. For instance, physical therapy is applied with no regard to the underlying physiology.
- NEMESIS experiments, irrespective of the hypothesis, will create rich and deep datasets that will be used for  years  to  come  to  address  many  other  questions.  For  instance,  our  Washington  University  stroke database has been downloaded hundreds of times by dozens of groups for independent research.
- NEMESIS represents the synergistic endeavour of large research groups led by one neurologist (*Corbetta*), one physiologist (Massimini), one cellular and system neuroscientist (Sanchez-Vives), and one physicist (Deco);  the  combination  of  methods  and  scale  of  analysis:  brain  imaging  and  behaviour  (Corbetta), neurophysiology  (Massimini),  cellular  and  systems  neuroscience  (Sanchez-Vives),  and  computational modelling (Deco); but, above all the integration of concepts and ideas. Overall, through the synergy of people,  technology,  and  concepts  NEMESIS  provides  a  true  'paradigm  shift'  for  understanding  and treating brain injuries.

Since Paul Broca's first description in 1861 of speech impairment after a frontal lesion, the dominant paradigm in  clinical  wards  and  Schools  of  Medicine  around  the  world  has  been  the neuron doctrine.  Accordingly, neurological impairment after focal injuries (e.g., stroke, trauma, epilepsy, tumours), but also degenerative (Alzheimer,  frontotemporal  dementia,  Parkinson),  or  developmental  (autism,  schizophrenia)  disorders,  are thought to reflect the dysfunction of local circuitries affected by the specific pathology. For instance, in the case of stroke, motor deficits or loss of speech arise because of dysfunction of neurons specialized for motor and language functions due to loss of blood circulation with secondary tissue hypoxia. This has led to the wellestablished idea that lesions in different parts of the brain give rise to different syndromes; that deficit and their recovery vary across individuals, and overall that stroke is a highly heterogeneous condition. Accordingly, treatments have been developed to solve the local issue. For instance, drugs or mechanical devices are used to re-open  occluded  blood  vessels,  while  exercises  (rehabilitation)  re-train  the  dysfunctional  neurons.  More generally, in clinical neuroscience across the board, treatments are delivered to patients while dealing with their central nervous system as a 'black box'. We use drugs, surgery, rehabilitation, and even non-invasive brain  stimulation  and  measure  their  effect  on  behaviour  with  little  or  no  consideration  of  how  different treatments influence brain signals, or vice versa how brain signals influence treatment.

The 1st pillar in NEMESIS is a new understanding of how focal lesions affect whole brain network function. It has been known for more than 100 years that focal lesions produce functional effects at a distance ("action à distance" 1 or "diaschisis" 2 ) -the dysfunction of remote regions connected to the site of injury. With the advent of neuroimaging, diaschisis was measured as the depression of perfusion/metabolic function, 3 or abnormalities of task evoked activity 4 in apparently normal brain areas connected to the injury. 5-7

About 10 years ago, Maurizio *Corbetta* and his team reported on a new pattern of diaschisis ('connectional diaschisis') consisting of the alteration of the temporal correlation

of the blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) signal measured with fMRI  between  brain  regions,  a.k.a.  'functional  connectivity' (FC)( Fig.2 ). 8,9 This  signal  is  a  more  direct  marker  of  abnormal communication between the lesion and connected distant regions. In the last ten years, they studied prospectively and longitudinally (1-2  weeks,  3  and  12  months)  hundreds  of  stroke  patients  with neurobehavioral  tests  and  structural,  diffusion,  perfusion,  and functional  MRI.  This  is  the  largest  stroke  cohort  in  the  world studied with multi-modal  imaging.  This  body  of  work  has established several new principles that we use in NEMESIS. (1) In contrast to what commonly believed, strokes damage predominantly  the  subcortical  nuclei  and  white  matter,  not cortex. In fact, only 15-20% of all strokes are cortical. Old school neurologists, before the advent of CT scan, knew that  focal  injuries  cause  a  slowing  of  the  EEG  signal  (delta frequency 0-4 Hz) that is maximal at the location of the lesion, but that is also present in the same hemisphere, or even in the opposite hemisphere ( Fig.4 ). This slowing can persist even at the chronic  stage.  These  clinical  observations  were  later confirmed  in  experimental  studies  in  animal  and  human models. 16-20 The key hypothesis of NEMESIS is that slow-wave activity  generated  near  the  lesion  spreads  to  directly  and indirectly connected regions impairing processing. Slow wave activity intrusion through the network represents the electrophysiological correlate of diaschisis and the mechanisms  responsible  for  functional  disconnection. This hypothesis  is  based  on  a  series  of  observations  obtained  by Marcello Massimini and his team that form the 2 nd pillar of NEMESIS. They developed a new approach to study changes of brain reactivity and connectivity across different brain states by employing direct cortical perturbations with TMS and EEG recordings. First, they found  that in different conditions characterized by loss of consciousness, including sleep ( Fig.5A )  and  anaesthesia,  the complex  response  to  TMS normally triggered  during  wakefulness invariably  collapses into  a  simple  slow  wave,  associated  with  loss  of  network complexity . 21-25 Using  intra-cranial  electrical  stimulation  and recordings ( Fig.5B ),  it  was found that slow waves reflect the tendency of cortical neurons to fall into a period of suppressed firing (OFF-period) after a transient increase in activity ('bistability' ). 26 Crucially,  cortical  bi-stable responses,  by  which neurons tend to 'hush and forget' upon receiving an input, also occur in focal injury conditions during wakefulness,  for example,  brain  injury  patients  in  vegetative  state 27 ( Fig.5C ). Key for our hypothesis, in a recent study (2021) they showed sleep-like cortical responses, characterized by OFF periods and low  complexity,  in  the  perilesional  cortex  near  a stroke ( Fig.5D ). 28 Furthermore, recent studies show that the intrusion

of  local  slow  waves  in  a  cortical  region  of  a  healthy  sleep-deprived  rodents 29 and  humans 3 correlate  with cognitive deficits specific to the cortical site. 30 Overall, these findings support the idea that slow wave activity after injury may lead to loss of network complexity and loss of function. Importantly, as discussed later in pillar 3, bi-stability and slow-waves represent a basic default mode of cortical activity that can be engaged by physiological and pathological alterations of the of the inhibition/excitation balance in neural circuits, 31 as well as by white matter lesions that disconnect cortex 32 . In conclusion, NEMESIS proposes that the intrusion of slow  waves,  and  the  underlying  cellular  mechanisms,  are  a  key  mechanism  of  network  disruption  and behavioural impairment after focal injury . Consequently, we posit that the suppression of pathological slow waves in brain injured subjects should be facilitating information processing hence function .

However, there is another side to the story. Slow waves during sleep by sending cortical circuits off-line exert a vital homeostatic regulation of synaptic excitability that promotes learning. 33 The loss of function that we experience every night during sleep is considered a price worth paying to have a more performant brain upon awakening. 34 Then, an intriguing possibility is that in the acute post-stroke phase local slow waves may be useful to preserve or restore the state of the residual circuitry for subsequent recovery. The presence of local slow  waves  may  be  still  related  to  global  dysfunction  through  anatomical  or  functional  disconnection. However, the reduced metabolic cost of slow wave activity 35 may be helpful to reduce excitotoxicity after brain  injury.  Overall, NEMESIS tests  the  hypothesis  that  sleep-like  slow  waves  are  the  physiological mechanism  of  disconnection  and  examines  their  role  in  inhibiting  information  processing  vs.  promoting plasticity. Correction  of  these  abnormalities,  however,  will  require  intimate  knowledge  of  the  underlying cellular events.

### Cellular origin and modulation of sleep-like slow wave activity (3 rd pillar)

A more thorough understanding of sleep-like slow waves requires a deeper dive into the cellular mechanisms to find out how they are generated, and how they can be manipulated either to dampen or promote their effect on network activity for the recovery of circuit function. Mavi Sanchez-Vives originally described the cellular and network underpinning of slow oscillations 36 and has been at the cutting edge of the field since, which is 3 rd pillar of NEMESIS.

Slow oscillations emerge from the recurrent interaction between cortical neurons, making them a network phenomenon. During the active periods, or Up-states, neocortical neurons (both excitatory and inhibitory) are depolarized, receive barrages of synaptic inputs and fire action potentials. During the ensuing Down-states or OFF-periods,  neurons  remain  hyperpolarized,  and  the  synaptic  activity  is  almost  silent. This  alternation resembles the 'bi-stability' described above in human studies. Cortical recordings from different species agree on the leading role of infragranular layers-layer 5-in the initiation of Up states. 36-38 During Up states, the activity expresses coherent oscillations at high frequencies in the beta (15-30 Hz) and gamma (30-90 Hz) range. This  phenomenon  is  scale-invariant  such  that  slow  oscillations  display  similar  characteristics whether they are recorded from the whole cortex of a sleeping human or from a small piece of cortex in a plate. This property reflects the dependency of slow oscillations on neural connectivity and the  *local activation* and regeneration of the waves sequentially along the network are the basis of slow wave propagation. 39-41

Sanchez-Vives and colleagues have proposed that fluctuations of Up- and Down-states -the origin of the slow waves-- are the 'default' intrinsic pattern of cortical activity . 42,43 The cortex defaults to slow-wave activity due to changes in neuromodulation, as in sleep, 44,45 due to changes in the excitatory/inhibitory balance as in deep anesthesia, 46 or because of structural lesions, as when a cortical gyrus is physically isolated by a cut of the underlying white matter 47 or in a cortical slab. 48 All these mechanisms are highly relevant for understanding how slow waves can be generated and then propagated after a stroke.

However, to gain insight on how cellular mechanisms impact network level phenomenology is necessary to develop comparable metrics across scales  (micro,  meso,  macroscale).  Since our  last  submission,  we  have worked hard to develop a common framework to describe changes in spontaneous activity and stimulus-evoked activity. We found that metrics of spontaneous activity (spectral exponent) and network complexity response

to stimulation (perturbation complexity  index,  PCI 49 )  similarly capture across models (slice, in-vivo rodents, humans) the difference between slow wave and normal desynchronized activity regime (Fig.6).

NEMESIS plans a systematic *exploration of the mechanistic basis* of slow wave generation and propagation  in  lesions  as  well  as *different neuromodulation strategies* *including electrical (non-invasive* and thalamic), pharmacology, photopharmacology  or optogenetics, to *induce a recovery of the complexity* *and processing capability of the* *cortical network.*

### Whole-brain models of healthy and lesioned brain networks (4 th pillar)

Gustavo Deco is one of the pioneers of whole-brain computational modelling, 50-52 an integrated strategy to simulate  multi-scale  signals  and  mechanisms  based  on  a  biologically  realistic  diagram  of  the  anatomical connectivity coupled with models of neuronal dynamics ( Fig.7 ). This is the 4th pillar of NEMESIS . The link between anatomical structure and functional dynamics, introduced more than a decade ago 53 is at the heart of whole-brain network models. The anatomy is represented by the structural connectivity (SC) of an individual  or  average  brain,  measured  in  vivo  by  diffusion  weighted  imaging  (DWI)  combined  with probabilistic tractography . 54-57 The spatial resolution is in the order of 1-2 mm, but with ultra-high field MRI resolutions 0.4 mm can be reached (Padova will install a 7T MRI system by 2024). The structural connectome describes the wire-diagram of the connections between cortical and subcortical regions as seen by tractography. The functional global dynamics result from the mutual interactions of local node dynamics coupled through the underlying empirical anatomical SC matrix . Whole-brain models aim to balance between complexity and realism  to  describe  the  most  important  features  of  the  brain in  vivo .  The  most  successful  whole-brain computational models have taken their lead from statistical physics where it has been shown that macroscopic physical systems obey laws that are independent of their mesoscopic constituents. The emerging collective macroscopic behaviour of brain models has been shown to depend only weakly on individual neurons. 58

### Animal models of stroke

NEMESIS envisages experiments in murine models of brain lesions in parallel to those in humans. A stroke can be induced experimentally by occlusion of a blood vessel or photo-thrombosis of cortex. Studies on murine models have mainly concentrated on small cortical lesions while human strokes are larger and disconnect predominantly the white matter. Thus far two main types of structural/functional modification of the neuronal circuits have been reported. First, during an acute 'critical period', perilesional cortex undergoes processes of local  synaptic  sprouting  with  the  re-configuration  of  local  connections,  and  angiogenesis.  This  local  reorganization in most cases is not capable alone, to support the recovery of the physiological function. 66,67 Second,  similarly  to  the  finding  in  humans, 9,12,68,69 a  functional  alteration  emerges  on  the  contralateral homologous region of the brain, likely via callosal disconnection, with a relative over-expression of glutamate receptors  and  excessive  response  to  stimuli  and  tasks.  The  notion  of  over-activity  in  the  contralateral hemisphere,  with  consequent  excessive  interhemispheric  inhibition  on  perilesional  cortex  (push-pull),  has guided  most  efforts,  but  as  earlier  reviewed  human  strokes  cause  widespread  patterns  of  disconnection. Currently network-wide abnormalities in murine subcortical strokes are largely unknown. 70,71

A stroke  model  in  living  rodents  represents  the  only  way  to  link  the  cellular  mechanisms  underlying  the intrusion  of  sleep-like  slow-waves  activity  to  structural-functional  disconnection  patterns  of  humans. Functional  imaging  at  the  population  and  brain-wide  scale  in  combination  with  electrophysiology  and optogenetics can address these open questions. Moreover, rodent models will be fundamental for a quantitative evaluation of the rehabilitative effects of interfering with slow wave dynamics. Rehabilitation re-organizes horizontal and thalamo-cortical connections with a functional remapping in neighbouring cortex, re-tuning of functional  properties  of  receptive  fields  and  re-focusing  of  cortical  patterns  toward  a  normal  condition. 72 Researchers  at  UNIPD  have  successfully  modulated  recovery  of  motor  function 72-74 with  a  series  of manipulation including robotic rehabilitation, optogenetic stimulation, and other neuro-modulation strategies.

### Rehabilitation and neurostimulation approaches for recovery of function

Traditionally  rehabilitation  has  employed  task-driven  exercises,  e.g.,  repetition  of  skilled  motor  tasks,  to improve recovery function after injury with weak-moderate efficacy. 75 Rehabilitation is applied with no regard to the underlying lesion characteristics and even less brain signals. More recently, there has been great interest in non-invasive brain stimulation (magnetic or electrical). However, a recent Cochrane analysis showed that repetitive TMS (rTMS) was not associated with a significant increase of activities of daily living or motor function . 76 Similarly, another Cochrane review on electrical stimulation (tDCS) did not find any effect on life skills, and a modest effect on upper limb function at the end of the intervention, but not 3 months later. 77 This dearth of effects reflects lack of understanding of the underlying mechanisms. There have been some promising results  with  closed-loop  stimulation, 78,79 both  invasively, 80,81 and  non-invasively. 82,83 There  have  been also recent  technological  developments  in  multi-site  TMS 84-86 that  could  be  very  helpful  in  the  context  of widespread disconnection. Connect-2-Brain, an ERC Synergy award, which Massimini and *Corbetta* advise, proposes the development of a multi-site TMS system coupled with high-density EEG for closed loop control.

### Beyond the state of the art

NEMESIS will provide the first complete characterization of the effects of focal lesions, both at the level of local circuits and brain networks, as measured with multi-modal imaging and electrophysiology (fMRI, DTI, EEG, multiphoton and mesoscale calcium imaging,  and high-density probes) in multiple systems (human patients, rodent lesions and stroke model, and local circuits in slice). NEMESIS will clarify the cellular and circuit  mechanisms  of  network  disconnection  after  focal  injury.  It  will  also  characterize  the  effects  of disconnection  on  the  responsiveness  of  brain  networks.  It  will  define  the  conditions  and  the  stimulation paradigms through which it is possible to rescue abnormal dynamics in affected circuits and restore the normal complexity  of  neuronal  responses  and  network  states.  NEMESIS  will  develop  generative  whole-brain computational  models  by  incorporating  cellular  and  mesoscopic  signals,  in  addition  to  classic  structural macroscopic signals, and guided by animal models, simulate the effect of different treatment protocols in terms of location and type of stimulation. Such models will guide proof-of-concept interventions that combine circuit stimulation and behavioural training.

### Objectives

NEMESIS has been organized in a set of high level inter-disciplinary objectives, each including multi-scale analysis that require the synergy of the four investigators. Each objective will be reached through a series of tasks that will generate the necessary information to move the project forward step-by-step toward the goals set out earlier.

- **Objective 1 (O1)** :  to  link post-stroke functional-structural disconnection with local and global sleep-like  brain  dynamics  in  different  lesion  models  (stroke,  thermocoagulation)  and  systems (humans, rodents, slice).
- Objective 2 (O2): to investigate and correct the mechanisms through which sleep-like slow-wave activity biases network activity at the whole brain level (human, rodents) and in slices.
- Objective 3 (O3): to develop predictive whole-brain computer models of individual patients or rodents  with  stroke;  to  simulate  different  stimulation  protocols  with  the  goal  of  normalizing abnormal  local  and  global  dynamics;  and,  to  use  this  information  for  optimizing  treatment protocols that combine stimulation with behavioural training.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- Task 1. Disconnection, slow waves, and behaviour in humans (*Corbetta*, Massimini, Deco)

Aim: To relate the patterns of lesion-induced disconnection measured with MRI with lesion-induced slow waves measured with EEG, we will measure simultaneously MRI and EEG signals in first time stroke patients sub-acutely (1-2 weeks) and chronically at 3 months, the period corresponding to the maximum behavioural recovery. 11 This  study  will  identify  whether  the  topography  of  structural  (SCD)  and  functional  (FCD) disconnection  matches  the  topography  of  slow  wave  activity,  or  whether  alternative  signals  explain connectional  diaschisis.  Our  strategy  for  circuit  stimulation  does  not  strictly  depend  on  the  slow  wave hypothesis. This study will also establish the most sensitive signals (SCD, FCD, EEG), or combination thereof, to track behavioural deficits and recovery. A behaviourally relevant biomarker is essential for circuit-based stimulation, and EEG signals would be much easier to obtain than MRI based signals. A scenario characterized by slow wave activity persistence at the chronic stage in patients who do not recover is consistent with a detrimental role of slow waves in information processing. In contrast, a positive relationship between acute slow-wave  activity  and  recovery  would  be  consistent  with  a  role  in  promoting  plasticity.  The  two  above scenarios are not mutually exclusive as it is conceivable that post-injury slow waves may be initially protective (in the acute phase) but then detrimental if they persist indefinitely (throughout the chronic phase).

Methods: N=60 first  time  stroke  patients  (age  18-90)  with  acute  neurological impairment  and  any  lesion location, will be studied at 1-2 weeks and 3 months with: Neuropsychology: Oxford Cognitive Screen, NIH stroke  scale,  Stroke  Impact  Scale,  and  NIH  toolbox.  We  will  extract  the  component  deficits  identified  in previous  studies  to  track  deficits,  recovery,  and  outcome.  Structural  MRI:  lesions  will  be  segmented  on T1/FLAIR  sequences.  Diffusion  MRI:  multi-directional  and  multi-weighted  diffusion  weighted  images. Probabilistic tractography will be run with spherical deconvolution to connect regions of interest based on RfMRI. Tract-specific measurements: volume, fractional anisotropy (FA) ( 9, 56 ),  and  Hindrance Modulated Orientational Anisotropy ( 57 ). Resting state fMRI: TE = 27 ms, flip angle = 90°, 3x3x3 mm isotropic voxels, volume  TR  =  2.00;  pairwise  normalized  Pearson  values  from  the  Schaefer-Yeo  atlas  parcellation  and subcortical  nuclei  after  pre-processing  including  motion  scrubbing.  High-density  EEG  (hd-EEG)nsi:  128channels, frequency bands: 0.01-100 Hz. In a subset of subjects, hd-EEG alone will be also acquired during sleep to assess changes in the overnight temporal dynamics of slow wave activity. As control group we will study N=30 age-education matched controls, typically family members of our patients.

Analysis: Structural-functional map of (altered) connectivity will be generated in each subject 12,87 . Hd-EEG data will be reconstructed in source space using cortical parcellation and thickness to constrain solutions. 88 The  presence  of  slow  waves  will  be  quantified  at  the  source  level  in  the  time  domain  (period-amplitude analysis) and frequency domain (spectral analysis and slope of the aperiodic components of the spectrum, i.e., the spectral exponent). Based on the hypothesis that slow waves increase near the stroke and in connected

areas, we will generate independent maps of SCD, FCD, EEG slow waves and compare them. We can also look for the EEG patterns  that  best  explain  structure-function  disconnection using  parcel-wise  regression  analysis.  These  signals  (SCD, FCD, EEG) can be input to machine learning models to predict behaviour  and  recovery.  Feasibility  is  established  by  new (October 2021) pilot studies ( Fig.8 ).

Extension 7T MRI: The University of Padova will acquire in  2024  a  new  generation  7T  MRI  scanner,  the  first  in Southern Europe. 7T  enhances: 1) the visualization of anatomical structures and connections (resolution of 0.2-0.4 mm comparable to ex-vivo resolution); 2) functional mapping of cortical layers; 3) temporal resolution of fMRI signals (600800 ms). We plan to scan a few selected cases of 'canonical' strokes,  e.g.,  cortical  vs.  white  matter  vs.  cerebellum,  to generate  state-of-the-art  maps  of  disconnection  and  whole brain models. By including separate parameters for laminar function,  high  resolution  white  matter  diffusion  mapping, cortical  variability  of  cellular  content  for  each  region,  and slow waves, we expect to improve the accuracy of whole brain model  prediction  (Task  6). Task 3. Stroke rodent model: disconnection and slow-wave activity (*Corbetta*, Sanchez-Vives, Deco)

Aim: The main goal is to develop a validated murine model of human stroke. First, we plan to develop and contrast cortical vs. subcortical models of stroke in terms of behaviour. Next, longitudinal electrophysiological and optical recordings from the perilesional and contralateral sides will allow to describe local activity and inter-hemispheric coupling during the entire post-stroke recovery phase. Third, we will perform whole brain optical imaging to map the spatial features of the slow wave topography. Fourth, these correlation approaches will  be  integrated  with  targeted  optogenetic  stimulation  to  compare  maps  of  intra-  and  inter-hemispheric activity and connectivity in resting and stimulated conditions.

Methods/Analysis: Stroke  in  adult  mice  will  be  induced  either  via  middle  cerebral  artery  occlusion (MCAO)(cortico-subcortical model) or via injection of  the  powerful  vasoconstrictor,  N5-(1-Iminoethyl)-lornithine (L-NIO) via a micropipette into the white matter, at three locations in rostral areas (pure subcortical model). 91 A battery of behavioural tests (e.g., grid walk test, Schallert cylinder) will be carried out before (baseline) and at different times after the stroke to comprehensively evaluate motor function. We will build a multiparametric motor score describing behavioural performance at different times after the lesion, to stratify ischemic  mice  based  on  'good'  and  'poor'  outcome.  Optical  recordings  will  be  carried  out  using  mice expressing a fluorescent Ca 2+ -based reporter of neuronal activity from the family of GCaMP6/7 molecules (GP5.5 Dkim/J. mice or via infection with AAVs). Based on this, brain wide activity over a field of view of 20 x20 mm 2  will be recorded at high frame rate with a CMOS camera, to reconstruct the patterns of neuronal activity in the proximity of the lesion, in perilesional cortex, and in the rest of the cortical surface. A seed-based  analysis of  the  correlation  patterns will  provide  *functional connectivity*  maps. The  whole  brain optical imaging studies will confirm the widespread pattern of disconnection and slow waves found in human subjects, specifically the low dimensionality of disconnection patterns and their behavioural relevance ( Fig.3 ). A  static  resting  state  analysis  will  be  complemented  with dynamic  and  directional  analysis  of  effective connectivity. For this, we will integrate the mesoscale imaging system with optogenetic stimulation relying on light sensitive modulators of neuronal activity in a combination with targeted illumination techniques. 92-94 The directionality of effects  is  very  important.  Do  inter-hemispheric  alterations  of connectivity primary or secondary to intra-hemispheric alterations ( Fig.3 )?  This  analysis  will  be  complemented  with longitudinal population activity recording by means of multiphoton  imaging  to  map  at  high  resolution in three dimensions the neuronal dynamics associated with slow wave activity following the induction of the stroke. 95,96 Neurophysiological recordings in the deeper structures will be conducted by implanting chronic electrodes in the two hemispheres, and recording LFPs longitudinally, before and at different times after stroke. ### Objective 2 (O2): to investigate the mechanisms through which local injury biases network activity at the whole brain level (human, rodents) and in cortical slices.

*Task 4. Human cortical stimulation of stroke networks (Corbetta, Massimini).*

Aim: While  task  3  develops  a  mouse  model  of  human  stroke,  and  employs  cortical  stimulation  to  assess network alterations from a causal perspective, task 4 brings this perturbational approach to human patients. We *will test the* idea that that the generation and propagation of slow waves in perilesional and remote portion of the cortex after focal injury are associated with an impairment of cortico-cortical information transmission and complexity, as measured by the dynamics of the EEG response to a direct perturbation with TMS. We plan to systematically stimulate with single pulse TMS perilesional cortex, contralateral homologous cortex, and other cortical regions that are directly or indirectly disconnected from the lesion, or that show or not slow wave activity ( Fig.8 ). Hd-EEG signals are recorded from the whole brain, and we plan to use metrics of spontaneous activity and stimulus-evoked activity recently validated across scales (spectral exponent and PCI, Fig.6 ). TMS will reveal local bi-stable responses and global reduced network response complexity not only near the lesion, but also in remote regions directly or indirectly connected to the lesion (preliminary results Fig.9 ). Further, we will test the causal link with behavioural impairment by performing longitudinal measurements in sub-acute and chronic patients. In patients in which abnormal stimulus-evoked responses persist chronically recovery will be less complete. The alternative hypothesis is that the presence of slow waves and abnormal stimulusevoked responses, acutely, represent a mechanism of homeostatic regulation of synaptic excitability. 33 Acute phase abnormalities may then correlate with chronic functional recovery.

Methods/Analysis: A new group of 60 stroke participants will undergo longitudinal assessments (1-2 weeks and 3 months) by means of combined TMS and hd-EEG recordings. We will obtain structural, diffusion, functional MRI to map the lesion and related structural/functional disconnection at the individual subject level. The relative maps of disconnection will be uploaded on the neuro-navigation system from which 5 sets of coordinates will be computed: perilesional, contralateral homologous, directly disconnected (n=1 connection from the lesion), indirectly disconnected (n>2 connections away from the lesion). TMS intensity will be based on  the  estimated-induced  electric  field  on  the  targeted  cortical  surface.  We  will  use  TMS  intensity  of approximately 110-120 V/m, which in previous TMS-EEG studies has been shown to be effective in eliciting EEG response in adults, 27 as  well  as  sham stimulation to control for unwanted sensory activations. 97 Each participant will undergo 200 TMS trials with inter-stimulus intervals randomized between 1.5-1.7 s. The whole session will last 1.5 hours, of which 60 min set up and 30 min stimulation. EEG will be recorded with a 124channel TMS-compatible amplifier (Bittium NeurOne, Kuopio, Finland). We will compute time-frequency plots, connectivity metrics based on source analysis and local and global complexity measures from the EEG

responses elicited from each TMS targeting site. 24,27,49 These perturbation-based electrophysiological indices of network interactions will be computed and compared in disconnected vs. non-disconnected regions of the brain, and compared to those obtained in 30 healthy controls stimulated in the same areas. The longitudinal assessment  of  perturbational  indices  of  bi-stability,  effective  connectivity  and  complexity  will  be  then correlated to behavioural recovery.

Outcomes: This study will provide (1) a map of the effect of stimulation from sites that show direct vs. indirect disconnection on local/global network responses; (2) a statistical model relating local/global network responses to stimulation and acute behavioural impairment/recovery. (3) Spontaneous and stimulus-evoked parameters (e.g., PCI) as input to whole brain models.

Task 6. Whole-brain models of disconnection and slow-waves (Deco, *Corbetta*, Massimini, Sanchez-Vivez).

Aim: The  goal  is  to  use  whole-brain  models  to  predict  the  effect  of  lesions  on  brain  networks  in  single individuals (human, rodent), and in different experimental paradigms (stroke, thermocoagulation). In addition, by introducing data from EEG or LFPs on slow waves, we will characterize the dynamics of slow waves, and how they propagate from the perilesional region to the rest of the network.

Methods/Analysis: The whole-brain human model combines structural neuroanatomical data (diffusion tensor imaging) and brain activity data (fMRI, hd-EEG, LFP). The global dynamics results from the mutual interactions of local node dynamics coupled through the underlying structural connectivity matrix. We

However,  here,  we  wish  to  implement  a  true  generative  predictive  model  of  the  effects  of  lesions  on connectivity. (1) For the *predictive model* , the whole-brain model will be fitted to the FC matrices of individual healthy  control  participants  and  to  the  group-averaged  FC  matrix  (separately,  we  can  test  Functional Connectivity  Dynamics  (FCD)  and  Probability  Metastable  States  (PMS)  fitting).  Model  fitting  will  be accomplished by varying both the global coupling parameter and effective SC parameters. The predicted FC matrix for each patient will then be generated by lesioning the fixed-parameter healthy control model via the individualized disconnection structure of each patient. We will induce sleep-like slow-waves in the damaged local regions by modifying the underlying conductivities (AMPA, NMDA and GABA) and rate adaptation level. 108 This model will have several improvements over previous models available. First, we will estimate the stroke damage based on structural connectivity disconnection, not only cortical damage as in previous studies. 109,110 *Corbetta* and his team have recently shown that SC disconnection is a much better prediction of FC  alterations  than  lesion  damage 13 :  directly  embedding  SC  disconnection  in  the  models  should  greatly increase their ability to estimate FC abnormalities. As in Griffis et al. 13 , we will embed the patient's segmented lesion into a healthy control structural connectome and determine the proportion of streamlines between each pair of regions that are interrupted by the lesion. This atlas-based procedure does not require patient-specific tracking of structural connections, but only the segmentation of the lesion that can be obtained through clinical scans. It can be validated with direct measures of SC disconnection based on diffusion imaging. Second, we will investigate the effect of slow-wave generation in the lesioned region and their propagation across the pathways of disconnection, to establish a mechanistic relationship between lesions, disconnection, and slowwave dynamics. (2) For modelling *the effect of thermocoagulation* , we will consider patient-specific wholebrain models built from his/her own  structural connectivity, lesion them at the location of the thermocoagulation, and induce sleep-like slow-waves. The model results will be directly compared with SEEG data empirically measured before/after coagulation. (3) The *rodent whole-brain model* will be constructed in similar ways but using detailed anatomy tracing studies (Allen Structural Connectome of mice) and fitting the generative parameters of the model (dynamical parameters and underlying effective connectivity) with the empirical calcium-based activity data and multisite neurophysiological recordings (spike activity and LFP). This animal model bridges non-invasive (fMRI, EEG) and invasive human data (SEEG) with neuronal activity and circuit level models.

Outcomes : A generative whole-brain model framework will offer a detailed causal mechanistic explanation of the relation between stroke damage and its propagation. A full characterization of the patterns of functional disconnection and sleep-like slow-wave dynamics is fundamental for understanding strategies for diagnosis, prediction, and most importantly stimulation-based recovery. These benefits will be further enhanced by high spatial (0.2-0.4 mm) and temporal (600-800 ms) resolution 7T MRI studies in Padova.

Another development would be to use task data to approximate a desired task state, which could be then reached through repeated stimulation and a closed-loop system. ### Task 8. Model-based rodent brain stimulation during rehabilitation (Corbetta, Deco, Sanchez-Vives)

Aim: Methods of stimulation studied in task 5, and strategies of stimulation developed through modelling in tasks 6-7 will be used to develop a protocol for treatment that integrates brain stimulation with rehabilitation. The goal will be to modulate slow waves (or alternative physiological mechanisms related to disconnection) either directly or indirectly through noise or high frequency stimulation, and normalize network complexity (PMS, PCI). We will focus on the physiological mechanism related to functional/structural disconnection since in human studies disconnection strongly relates to the degree of behavioural impairment and recovery.  First, we *will test the* effect of stimulation on-line in a motor task. Then, the treatment will be applied chronically over  several  sessions  paired  with  motor  rehabilitation  delivered  by  a  robotic  device  with  which  UNIPD researchers have extensive experience. 72-74

Methods: Infarcts will be caused via MCAO in adult mice separating cortical from subcortical. Stimulation will be carried out via either optogenetic approaches or electrical micro-stimulation before or in phase with movement execution. Optogenetic neuromodulation will drive identified nodes within the whole brain network with activity oscillations at specific spectral bands. We will express the light sensitive depolarizing channel ChR2 in pyramidal neurons or fast spiking interneurons (Parvalbumin-positive) for modulating slow and fast rhythms respectively. This high-resolution neuromodulation strategy, in combination with brain wide optical recordings represent the closest approximation to the stimulation scenario evaluated with the model. A second approach  is  frequency-locked  electrical  micro-stimulation  strategy  that  has  given  encouraging  results  in preliminary unpublished work. Mice display a dramatic reduction in skilled reaching abilities post-stroke. 74,111 We *will test the* hypothesis that appropriate stimulation modulating slow waves (before or during movement execution)  enhances  on-line  grasping  precision.  The  same  approach  will  be  applied  chronically,  in  daily rehabilitation sessions post-stroke, to determine whether it leads to functional gains that persist beyond the treatment period and generalize to non-trained sensory-motor tests.

Outcomes: This  task  will  test  the  effectiveness  of  novel,  model-based  protocols  of  modulation  and rehabilitation aiming at restoring connectivity and network complexity during motor training in rodents.

### Task 9. Model-based human brain stimulation during rehabilitation (Corbetta, Massimini, Deco)

Aim: This task will strongly depend on previous experiments. The goal is to create a stimulation protocol in parallel with rehabilitation to improve disconnection and dynamics post-stroke. We plan to test the hypothesis that modulation of slow wave activity (or other mechanisms of disconnection) by non-invasive stimulation improves  transiently  (on-line)  performance.  When  given  chronically  in  multiple  sessions  paired  with rehabilitation it shall improve recovery.

Methods/Analysis: The protocol will be detailed after a thorough evaluation of preliminary results in rodents, different  kinds  of  whole  brain  models,  and  exploratory  analyses  using  TMS/EEG.  Here  we  discuss  some choices and trade-offs. *Stroke patients* . We plan to study chronic first-time stroke patients with stable deficits. Positive effects on stable symptoms would be the most convincing since it would be net of any confound of plasticity. Alternatively, stronger effects may occur in sub-acute patients when plasticity mechanisms are most active in the first three months post-stroke. This approach, however, would require a sham control group. Design. Multiple session-single subject design with multiple baseline assessments prior to treatment. Lesions *and deficits:* We seek a strong effect size (Cohen d>0.5). We plan to use patients with right MCA lesions with significant motor deficits and visuospatial neglect. Behavioural and functional deficits will be measured as in our previous studies 10,11 . *Technique for stimulation.* The protocol will be selected based on the animal work and  whole  brain  modelling  with  the  goal  of  modulating  slow  waves  (or  other  behaviourally  relevant mechanisms). Several options will be explored: (1) inhibitory or excitatory repetitive TMS (rTMS) to enhance or dampen slow-wave activity, 22 either locally or multiple locations. There is evidence for the efficacy of theta burst stimulation to left parietal cortex in patients with visuospatial neglect. 112 This protocol is currently applied clinically targeting the EEG electrode P3 with no regard to the underlying pattern of disconnection or dynamic abnormalities. Accordingly, significant variability in response has been reported 113 . We strongly believe that at a minimum our work will permit to select the best site of stimulation based on the disconnection signal (e.g., SCD, FCD, slow waves) by abating individual variability hence giving a more stable efficacy; (2) transcranial direct  or  alternating  current  stimulation  (tDCS,  tACS)  to  inject  anodal  or  cathodal  currents  vs.  noise  or oscillatory responses, as in the preliminary slice data ( Fig.10 ). Current commercial systems can stimulate 1-2 locations,  multi-site  stimulation  may  be  coming  on-line  in  the  next  5  years.  (3)  EEG-guided,  closed-loop stimulation on specific phases of slow waves, as in recent work and the Synergy grant Connect-2-Brain, 114 in which  *Corbetta*  and  Massimini  are  members  of  the  scientific  board. Loci  of  stimulation: based  on computational and animal models. *Behavioural protocols:* As in rodents (task 8) we will run a reaching motor task to natural objects with the hemiparetic vs. normal arm. Arm movements will be tracked with cameras and electromyography. In these conditions patients with moderate hemiparesis cannot reach accurately or cannot shape accurately their hand. The task will be continuous to emphasize time-on task. During 'reaching without stimulation', we will first characterize the occurrence of transient slow-wave activity in relation to movement. Subsequently, we can apply 'stimulation' either prior or in phase of the movement or time-locked to the slowwave activity on some random trials. We predict that motor performance (direction, accuracy) will be worse on trials in which slow waves are stronger in motor cortex (or other disconnected areas), similarly to Andrillon et al. 30 Modulation of slow waves will transiently improve performance. A second protocol will record eye movements during a natural visual exploration task or goal-directed search tasks to low saliency targets. In these conditions patients with attention deficits explore the right side of the visual scene ignoring items on the left. Again, we will run a study in which we measure the on-line relationship between lateralized dynamic slow-wave transients in the parietal cortex and abnormal deviations of eye movements. Then, we can run rehabilitation protocols with stimulation.

*Read-out and feedback:* To stabilize behaviour, feedback may be important. Three read-out signals may be used: (1) motor/attention performance; (2) peripheral signals (EMG, trajectories, eye movements); (3) central signals  (brain  connectivity  states;  slow  waves;  etc.).  This  information  can  be  given  to  the  patient  in  a

biofeedback  paradigm.  A  more  complex  strategy  (the  one  of Connect-2-Brain) is to run closed-loop where the feedback is based on the similarity between the patient's data and a model computed from the healthy controls.

Outcomes: We  will  test  different  strategies  for  intervention  to improve function in stroke patients. We believe these experiments are  important  as  they  provide  the  state-of-the-art  on  circuit-based stimulation in humans. Even though an effective treatment may not be achieved, this work is fundamental to the whole idea of circuitbased stimulation. As such these experiments are critical for the field.

ICREA is a foundation supported by the Catalan Government and guided by a Board of Trustees which aims to recruit top scientists for the Catalan R&D system: scientists capable of leading new research groups, strengthening existing groups, and setting up new lines of research. IDIBAPS  has  a  formal  relationship  with  ICREA,  who  will  provide  third-party  in-kind  resources  (Prof. Sanchez-Vives salary) to IDIBAPS. Although the salary costs of Prof Sanchez-Vives are paid by ICREA, she is assigned to physically work at IDIBAPS and considered a member of IDIBAPS. Dr Sanchez-Vives will be the coordinator of IDIBAPS research in NEMESIS.
