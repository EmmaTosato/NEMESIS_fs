### Letter to the Editor

### Resting-state functional connectivity guides motor hotspot localization

A R T I C L E  I N F O

Keywords: TMS-EEG fMRI

Large-scale networks

Neurostimulation

### Dear Editor

Functional  MRI  (fMRI)  is  emerging  as  a  promising  surrogate  for neurostimulation target selection in basic and clinical research (Soleimani, et al., 2023). While studies combining task-based fMRI and transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) motor mapping have shown high correspondence between task-evoked activation and electrophysiological responses (Sarfeld, 2012) -this overlap is not clear for restingstate functional connectivity (rsFC). rsFC offers a more accessible and standardized acquisition protocol and can be applied to brain regions that cannot be probed through standardized activation paradigms (i.e., task fMRI) or that lack direct neurophysiological readouts. However, the source of bias introduced by fMRI remains debated; studies comparing targeting methodologies are lacking.

In this study, we compared primary motor cortex target selection based on either conventional motor evoked potentials (MEPs) or rsFCderived target selection acquired separately from the same individual (Cavaleri et al., 2017). To assess the functional correspondence of the identified hotspots, we employed the electroencephalographic response to TMS (TMS-EEG) as our neurophysiological readout.

We recruited ten healthy participants (age = 25 ± 4.2) each of whom underwent  both  resting-state  fMRI  and  a  separate  TMS-EEG  session. fMRI data were preprocessed using an in-house pipeline (see **Supplementary Material**: Imaging acquisition and pre-processing) . During TMS-EEG, participants were stimulated at two cortical targets: the right motor hotspot identified through conventional MEP-based localization (i.e. TMS-EMG targeting the APB hotspot with the largest/most consistent MEP) and the right rsFC peak identified using the MEP-derived left motor  hotspot  in  a  seed-based  correlation  analysis.  100  single  TMS pulses were delivered to each stimulation target at an intensity of 120 % resting motor threshold (RMT) with a posterior to anterior orientation (mean ± SD stimulator output: 58.3 ± 2.97 %). Pulses were delivered with  a  randomly  jittered  interstimulus  interval  (1500 -1700  ms).To minimize auditory evoked potentials, a masking white noise was used throughout the stimulation (Ter Braack et al.). TMS targeting precision was ensured with a Navigated Brain Stimulation (NBS) system. TMSEEG data were preprocessed using an in-house pipeline (see Supplementary  material:  TMS-EEG  pre-processing paragraph). Spatially,  the  Euclidean  distance  between  MEP-  and  FC-derived hotspots  was  negligible  ( < 5  mm;  mean ± SD = 4.1 ± 2.6  mm) ( Fig. 1 B). A repeated measures MANOVA (dependent variables: x -y -z coordinates of MEP- and FC-derived sites) showed no significant differences  (Wilks-Lambda = 0.910,  p = 0.713).  Standard-space  coordinates of each subject are provided in the Supplementary Material (Fig. S1).

Fig. 1 A shows that TEPs following stimulation of the rsFC-derived hotspot  were  similar  to  those  evoked  by  the  MEP-derived  hotspot (group-averaged butterfly plots and TEPs topographies). The stimulation of the motor hotspots elicited high-amplitude lateralized EEG responses. Well-described peaks following M1 stimulation (including P30, P60 and N100) were clearly identifiable across conditions. Signal topographies across time were localized to the motor system and were highly similar between the two conditions. As expected, MEPs elicited from the rsFC-derived hotspot were slightly smaller and less consistent than those from the MEP-defined site.

Similarities were tested across several neurophysiological measures. No significant differences were found in the local mean field potential (LMFP) within the first 200 ms (t = 0.345, p = 0.84) or in the number of significant  deflections  (t = 1.000,  p = 0.332).  Interhemispheric propagation (t = 0.273, p = 0.788) and interhemispheric balance (t = 0.495, p = 0.627) were comparable across conditions. Perturbation Complexity Index state transition (PCIst) values were also equivalent (t = 0.703,  p = 0.492) ( Fig.  1 C). Source-level  analyses  using  a nonparametric  cluster-based  permutation  test  corrected  for  multiple comparisons revealed no significant differences between conditions (see Supplementary  Material:  Source  reconstruction  and  statistical analysis ).

Overall, these results indicate that rsfMRI-based targeting provides a reliable  alternative  for  identifying  stimulation  sites  within  the  motor cortex. The similarity across neurophysiological measures, both local (LMFP)  and  distributed  (interhemispheric  spatial  propagation  (ISP), PCIst), supports the hypothesis that the hand motor area can be effectively identified by both techniques. In addition, the functional correlation between the left MEP-derived hotspot and the right FC-derived *Letter to the Editor* MRI derived hotspot, is highly consistent with the anatomical organization of the motor system. The corpus callosum, particularly the anterior and midbody regions,  contains  dense  transcallosal  fibers  connecting  homologous motor regions in both hemispheres (Ruddy et al., 2017).

While our results highlight that functionally connected motor areas elicit similar cortico-cortical responses, future work should extend this investigation to other brain regions. Evidence suggests that homologous transcallosal  connectivity  extends  beyond  the  motor  system,  encompassing prefrontal, parietal, and temporal networks. In this context, our findings are particularly relevant for brain areas lacking direct neurophysiological readouts (e.g., MEPs) where resting-state fMRI may provide critical spatial information for targeting. Moreover, these results have implications for populations in which MEPs are absent or unreliable -such as stroke, neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., amyotrophic lateral sclerosis), or peripheral neuropathies (Stinear et al., 2012). In such cases, traditional motor hotspot mapping becomes challenging due to the lack of consistent electrophysiological responses. In stroke patients with severe corticospinal tract damage, for example, MEP identification  can  be  difficult.  This  consideration  is  particularly  relevant since stroke pathology often affects subcortical regions (Corbetta, 2015) and is accompanied by substantial neuroplastic reorganization of the sensorimotor network (Ward, 2006). In this framework, single-subject rsFC  mapping  could  represent  a  promising  approach  to  identify  and stimulate spared functional networks.

Some limitations should be noted. First, the sample size was limited. Second, the use of a high, standardized, stimulation intensity may have affected  the  results.  This  increases  the  induced  electric  field,  thus affecting the specificity of TMS-induced cortical activation. Moreover, the risk of introducing sensory co-stimulation (peripheral proprioceptive  reafference  and  scalp  activations)  is  increased.  Future  studies should employ real-time TEP monitoring to titrate stimulation parameters based on individual cortico-cortical properties (Casarotto, 2022).

Taken  together,  our  findings  suggest  rsFC  provides  a  robust  and effective method for TMS targeting. We demonstrate a good spatial and functional correspondence to MEP-derived approaches for motor hotspot  localization.  This  methodology  holds  promise  for  applications beyond the motor cortex and in scenarios where MEP-based localization is unreliable. Future work should compare rsFC-derived targeting with anatomical landmark mapping and indirect FC estimation (Fox, 2018), to shed light on the most effective strategy to identify targets suitable for stimulation beyond the motor cortex.
