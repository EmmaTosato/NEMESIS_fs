### Letter to the Editor

### Detecting cortical reactivity alterations induced by structural disconnection in subcortical stroke

Focal vascular brain lesions not only affect areas directly involved by the insult, but also structurally connected regions. This phenomenon, known as diaschisis (von Monakow, 1914), causes functional alterations beyond the site of structural damage which contribute to patients' behavioral impairment and functional recovery.

From an electrophysiological standpoint, a slowing of the electroencephalographic (EEG) activity during wakefulness is classically reported in the area surrounding focal cortical -but not subcortical- infarcts. Empirical (Sarasso et al., 2020) and modeling (Cattani et al., 2023) studies suggest that these alterations can be best revealed by probing cortical reactivity through direct cortical stimulations.

Here, we test the ability of transcranial magnetic stimulation combined with EEG (TMS-EEG) coupled with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-derived quantitative estimates of structural disconnection (SDC; (Foulon et al., 2018) in detecting functional alterations in a case of subcortical stroke. Specifically, we hypothesize that probing cortical areas distant from the lesion with TMS would show spatially-specific EEG reactivity alterations predicted by SDC maps.

A man in his 50 0 s was admitted to the emergency room (ER) for acute onset weakness and paresthesia of the right upper and lower limb. On ER arrival, his National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS; (Brott et al., 1989) score was 1 (minor facial motor deficit of the right lower face). A brain computerized tomography (CT) and CT angiography did not reveal acute focal lesions or large vessel occlusions. During the first day of clinical observation, he worsened, and the day after, NIHSS score was 5 (right hemiparesis and ataxia, persistence of the minor facial paresis, drift of the upper and lower limb, slightly dysarthric speech and reduced right hand grip and coordination). On a new brain CT scan acquired the day after symptoms onset, a hypodense area at the border between the left thalamus and the posterior limb of the internal capsule was visible. The lesion was then documented on MRI (transverse maximum diameter: 1.3 cm, antero-posterior maximum diameter: 2.4 cm; Fig. 1A). Diagnostic procedures excluded cardioembolic or thromboembolic origin of the stroke, and the event was eventually classified as cryptogenic. The patient did not suffer from previous stroke nor was affected by other neurological or psychiatric disorders but had a myocardial ischemia 4 years before. He did not report any pre-stroke disability as assessed by the modified Rankin Score (mRS; (van Swieten et al., 1988) = 0.

To assess cortical reactivity, we performed a TMS-EEG recording on day 10 after symptoms onset (NIHSS score = 5). To guide the

selection of TMS cortical targets, the patient's lesion was manually segmented from the T1-weighted MRI, non-linearly registered to a normative space and used as seed to compute the SDC map. The SDC map was then back-projected into the subject MR native space as in (Foulon et al., 2018). We thus identified a first TMS target within the cortical region with the highest SDC probability (corresponding to the left Brodmann Area (BA) 4). TMS was also targeted to the right BA4 as well as the left BA6 as contralesional and ipsilesional control areas, respectively (Fig. 1B).

For each cortical target, TMS pulses were delivered with a focal figure-of-eight coil driven by a Mobile Stimulator Unit (eXimia TMS Stimulator, Nexstim Ltd.) guided by a Navigated Brain Stimulation (NBS) system (Nexstim Ltd.).

EEG responses to TMS were recorded with a 62-channels, TMScompatible BrainAmp DC amplifier (Brain Products GmbH, Germany) with DC-to-1-kHz hardware filtering bandwidth, 5-kHz sampling rate and acquisition reference electrode placed on the forehead. Prior to the TMS-EEG recording, we acquired 5 minutes of resting EEG with eyes open using the same acquisition parameters. Power spectral density (PSD) estimates were computed for each channel using the Welch's method (2-s Hanning windows, 50% overlap) and averaged across bins pertaining to the classical EEG frequency ranges (Delta: 0.5-4 Hz, Theta: 5-8 Hz; Alpha: 812 Hz; Beta: 12-20 Hz; Gamma: 20-40 Hz). All EEG preprocessing steps are described in (Sarasso et al., 2020).

TMS-evoked potentials (TEPs) recorded from the SDC-informed target (left BA4) showed a pathological response, characterized by a high-amplitude EEG slow wave similar to those described over perilesional areas of focal cortical lesions (Sarasso et al., 2020). Conversely, TEPs recorded in both control areas (left BA6 and right BA4) showed the typical waveforms characterized by a site-specific recurrent set of voltage oscillations (Fig. 1C). Spontaneous EEG was devoid of lateralized focal low-frequency activity over the scalp EEG derivations overlaying the SDC map cortical ''hot-spot' (Fig. 1D), as illustrated by both delta and theta PSD topographies (Fig. 1E).

Combining TMS-EEG with SDC allows detecting functional alterations distant from focal subcortical structural lesions spatially predicted by the SDC map and otherwise absent in spontaneous EEG activity. These findings carry two main implications.

Firstly, quantitative estimates of SDC may serve valuable a priori for uncovering neurophysiological changes in cortical areas far from anatomical lesions. This aligns with the classical concept of diaschisis (von Monakow, 1914), where functional changes in circuits distant from structural lesions contribute to cognitive/behavioral impairments and may play a role in the recovery process.

Second, in line with previous studies (Sarasso et al., 2020), this report confirms that direct cortical perturbations can reveal cortical functional alterations that cannot be immediately appreciated in spontaneous activity. Subcortical brain lesions can result in

According to a recent modeling study (Cattani et al., 2023), the ensuing increase of adaptation mechanisms and shift in the excitation/inhibition balance can lead to significant changes in the input/output properties of cortical circuits. These alterations can be effectively revealed by direct perturbations as the occurrence of high-amplitude slow responses like those observed during sleep. Our findings of a similar alteration at the cortical site most affected by SDC is consistent with this modeling prediction.

Overall, combining TMS-EEG with SDC allows a systematic mapping of the electrophysiological alterations over widespread cortical areas and usefully complement other neuroimaging tools in the assessment of the functional consequences of focal brain injury.
